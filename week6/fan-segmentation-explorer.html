Week 6 · Explorer

Fan segmentation explorer

Every dot is one of the 1,200 fans in the Biscayne Bay Sports Group database. Watch a computer sort them into groups that behave alike, step by step, then read each group's profile and the campaign it suggests.

How to use this interactive
  1. Choose how many groups you want (2 to 6) and press "Place starting points". The computer drops that many group centers on random fans.
  2. Press "Next step" to watch the two moves repeat: every fan joins the nearest center, then every center moves to the middle of its fans.
  3. Keep stepping, or press "Run to the end", until no fan changes group. The groups are now stable.
  4. Read the group profiles below the chart and compare the suggested campaigns.
  5. Try "New starting points" or a different number of groups. Do the groups still make business sense?

This method is called k-means clustering ("k" is the number of groups). Games attended and spending are first put on the same 0 to 1 scale, so a difference of $100 does not count more than a difference of 10 games. Data: fans.csv (fictional).

What is happening

Press "Place starting points" to begin.
Round 0Fans who changed group 0Spread inside groups -

"Spread inside groups" adds up how far each fan is from the center of their group. It can only go down or stay the same with each step. When it stops changing, the groups are as tight as they will get from these starting points.

Why a manager cares

One message for every fan wastes money. A family that comes twice a year needs a different offer than a supporter who never misses a game. Segments let marketing match the offer, the channel, and the timing to each group.

Group profiles

Group names and campaigns are suggestions based on each group's averages. A real marketing team would test them.
